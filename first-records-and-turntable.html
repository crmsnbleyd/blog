Waxing poetic about my records and turntable

Posted on Sep 25, 2026

Music in the streaming age is weightless. It reaches you through the aether from servers thousands of miles away into whatever speaker happens to be nearby. It seems almost infinite, is completely free of friction, and incredibly easy to take for granted.

I recently bought a record player and my first three records, with plans to buy more.

To anyone used to tapping a glass screen to hear any song ever recorded, this looks like too much work to listen to a limited number of tunes. Records are bulky, inconvenient, require physical storage, and only hold about twenty minutes of music per side before you have to get up and flip them over.

Yet, much like taking the time to manually grind whole coffee beans instead of stirring instant powder into boiling water, the friction adds to its appeal.

The instant coffee vs. freshly brewed coffee analogy breaks down when it comes to quality though. Any coffee connoisseur will say brewed coffee tastes better than instant (due to a variety of reasons), but standard CD-quality digital audio technically has higher fidelity and dynamic range than vinyl ever will. Records degrade over time due to dust and scratches, leading to sound artifacts (which I am coming to terms with), and cost a ridiculous amount of money in India, thanks to import duties and a weak rupee.

What makes a record “worth it”, still

I’m sort of a minimalist and never thought I’d have a collection of anything, but I love music and hate how exploitative streaming is (more on this later), so it makes complete sense to have a little record collection.

Here are some reasons I started one, in no particular order.

Difference from digital media

Unlike digital files which store sound as a collection of bits, a vinyl record is a direct physical artifact of audio.

When a record is pressed, soundwaves are literally etched into a single continuous groove on a sheet of PVC plastic. If you zoomed in with a microscope, you'd see the actual undulating shape of the soundwaves themselves.

It’s such a delicate, magical, yet simple thing, and it boggles my mind that this exists at all.

Relative rarity

Vinyl records went out of fashion because of their cost of production, fragility, and low portability, and everyone streams, so it makes the experience even more interesting, as I didn’t get to listen to any until recently. You did however see them in all sorts of media, especially that from the heyday of records, which added to the mystique.

The art looks really pretty

The sleeve, of course, has the album art, which is usually pretty enough on its own, but they also quite commonly have things like lyrics booklets,

Records themselves look good as well

Our first record, ali by Vieux Farka Touré and Khruangbin, was a translucent blue-green instead of the usual black, which was such a pleasant surprise.

Another record I purchased, “Is This It” by The Strokes, is a limited editiion translucent red.

Plain black records also look great, though.

You might enjoy the music more

The ritual of pulling the disc from its sleeve, cleaning off the dust, gently lowering the tonearm, and watching it spin anchors your attention to the room. It turns listening into a dedicated activity rather than background noise.

Dust and cat hair gets on the record, which causes pop sounds, so one has to clean it, and the act of moving it to and from the sleeves and the turntable creates static, which makes a sort of buzzing sound. You really have to love a record to want to go through all of that. It connects you to the activity of listening instead of being a passive participant, and I guess the work makes you appreciate it more?

Streaming pays very little

The artist gets more money. If you buy a record in India, maybe 10% of the sale price has actually gone to the artist (because the retailer, the government, the publisher, etc all take their cut), but it takes 100,000 to 150,000 streams on Spotify for an artist to net the same amount. Streaming is hell for smaller artists especially, and is not enough to make a living.

A section of Wikipedia on Spotify’s compensation practices

More miscellaneous reasons

  • My partner’s cousins and brother have one.
  • It’s a physical display of your music tastes.
  • Actually owning your music.
  • Records and the turntable and other equipment look really good in a room, and you can talk about it.

Neighbourhood record store

I live quite close to this cute record store called On the jungle floor. They’ve got a massive collection of records and the staff is friendly. I love visiting there to just look at the really expensive equipment they have and drool at the LPs they have.

Two of the records we’ve bought have been from here!

An interesting thing I heard after a couple of conversations there with people either working or hanging out there as friends of the owner, people volunteer there for free for some reason? Not sure why anyone would do that other than as a favour to a friend. If any reader has done something like this for a for-profit business, please let me know.

There was a guy behind the counter who wasn’t an empployee. He’s a DJ and was playing some of his personal records from a massive tote bag full of records, trying to figure out what to play on his next set. Maybe he was getting the most out of the ₹3L turntable. I didn’t get to talk to him too much, but my partner really liked the LPs he had, and since we can’t find them for sale anywhere in India, this has caused a lot of consternation. For anyone who want to check out his music, his name is Saksham, @ghewxr on Instagram.

The current rig

I’m using an almost entry-level turntable from

I just use a portable bluetooth speaker via aux at the moment because dumping all my disposable income into a new hobby would be silly. Even when I do get speakers, I’m going for the (relatively) cheaper end for now. I’m not an audiophile, and thank god for that. Like every hobby there’s a way to dump all your money into this. After a certain point, you get diminishing returns and are just trying to optimise the fun out of your equipment.

I’m thinking of doing listening parties, though, and the idea of bringing people over to listen to music coming out of a cheap portable speaker does seem a bit sad. Down the line, the goal is to set up a proper stereo amplifier connected to a decent pair of passive bookshelf speakers, giving the physical media room to actually push some air. The bass on the Strokes album goes hard, so a subwoofer is also on the cards.

Thinking of getting Polk Audio Monitor XT15 Compact Bookshelf Speaker from avstore.in alongside the Fosi Audio V3 amplifier. This will go great with my hiby music player as well, letting me listen to digital music from a more “intentional” source than streaming.
