How and where to invest your money

Posted on Aug 03, 2026

This is not intended to be a comprehensive guide to investing, just a few basics I think everyone should know, and maybe dispel a little bit of the fear people might feel when thinking about investing.

So you’ve saved some money from your pay-cheque. This is a great accomplishment, but just keeping it in a low-interest savings account is probably not a good idea. Money that is left idle, that is, when it’s not invested in any way, loses value over time. The same amount of currency in 2000, let’s say $500, bought much more stuff than it can in 2026. There are exceptions like electronics getting cheaper over time, or certain markets at certain times like in Japan, but for a standard mix of everyday goods (housing, food, energy), prices trend upward, a phenomenon called inflation.

While most savings accounts do pay you a little on a regular basis for the privilege of being able to use your money to give out loans and invest, which is how the banks make money, it’s a low rate when compared to the popular ways to investing in stocks or bonds, and usually does not beat the rate of inflation.

Prerequisites

We need to work out if investment makes sense for the situation you’re currently in. - Emergency Funds. These can be for any unforseen - Pay down high interest debt: Clear anything with an interest rate above 8–10% before investing in equity markets. Low-interest, tax-advantaged loans (like home loans) can co-exist with long-term investing, but high-cost consumer debt will dismantle your compounding curve faster than the market can build it. Always pay as much as you can to save on interest. There’s tax on money earned but not on money saved. - insurance

Mutual Funds

  • Index funds or passive funds (explanation here)
  • The costs or a Mutual Fund are what you are paying over your actual investments. It’s like a tax on whatever you invest, and it reduces the return of the fund. Some costs are expense ratio: which is used to compensate the firm running the mutual fund, exit load: a percentage you pay if you take your money out before a certain period (to incentivise you to keep your money for longer), tracking error: only matters for index funds and measures how closely to an index the fund’s price movements correspond.
  • Use a Direct Mutual Fund using an investing app like Zerodha or Groww. Your bank app might show mutual funds but they are almost always Regular, with high costs
  • ETFs exist

Asset classes

Gold

Gold is traditionally seen as a great store of wealth, but it does not grow in value during boom times like stocks do. The idea is that as inflation increases, gold and other precious metals are still inherently worth what they were a few decades ago, since nothing has actually changed about them in that time. There will probably always be people who want to buy gold, and so it’s a safe place to store your money. I don’t recommend holding more than 5-10% of your net worth in gold, and here are my reasons: - It is quite volatile for an asset that is supposed to keep your money safe. The price goes up and down erratically based on general mood, even if nothing about gold itself has changed.

If you are to invest in gold: - Do not buy jewelry for investment, as there’s taxes and making charges - At least in India, there are taxes on gold bullion and "Digital gold" as well

The best option is to pick a gold mutual fund/ETF with a low expense ratio.

Stocks (Equities)

Investing in stocks means owning small parts (shares) of real companies. These companies sell products or services, employ people, build technology, and (hopefully) generate profits. They also own physical assets like land, factories, and inventory, along with intangible ones like patents and brand reputation.

Owning a share entitles you to a claim on those underlying assets and future earnings. That claim is what gives a stock its fundamental value.

  • The Stock-Picking Trap: Trying to pick individual winning stocks is extraordinarily difficult for retail investors. You are competing against institutional funds with armies of analysts, proprietary algorithms, and optical data feeds that execute trades in microseconds. For most people, stock picking turns into expensive gambling.
  • Baskets Over Bullets: Instead of trying to guess which single company will win, buy a large basket of stocks. Through index funds, you can own a slice of the top 50, 500, or 1,000 companies in an economy all at once. If a few companies fail, the broader basket absorbs the loss while benefiting from the overall growth of the economy.
  • Different Kinds of Baskets:
  • Large-Cap Funds: Baskets of massive, well-established companies (like Apple, Reliance, or HDFC). They offer stability and steady growth.
  • Mid/Small-Cap Funds: Baskets of younger or smaller companies. They carry higher failure rates, but offer much higher growth potential if they scale up.
  • Sector Funds: Baskets focused on a single industry (like Technology or Banking). These carry high concentration risk because an entire industry can suffer at the same time.
  • The Risk vs. Return Trade-Off: Equities offer the highest long-term historical returns of any major asset class, but that return is the compensation you receive for enduring volatility. In the short term, stock prices swing wildly based on earnings reports, global events, and human emotion. In the long term, however, prices follow company earnings—making broad stock market index funds consistent performers.
